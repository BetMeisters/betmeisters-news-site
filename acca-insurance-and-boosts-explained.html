Acca insurance and odds boosts explained: what they are worth, and how to check one

Bookmakers use acca insurance and price boosts to make accumulators look better value. Here is how each works, what the small print usually says, and a simple way to work out what an offer is really worth.

FootballSports betting
By the BetMeisters.news desk20 November 20267 min read

Two offers dominate accumulator betting in the UK: acca insurance (your stake back, usually as a free bet, if one leg lets you down) and odds boosts (a bigger price on a selection or a whole acca). Both are real extras, but neither changes the basic maths of an accumulator. This guide explains how they work and how to put a number on them. 18+.

First, why accas are hard to win

An accumulator multiplies the prices of its legs together, and it also multiplies the bookmaker's margin. If each leg carries a margin of about 5%, a five-leg acca carries roughly 1.05 multiplied by itself five times, or about 28% on top of the fair price. That is why accas pay big when they land but lose money over time for most bettors. Insurance and boosts are designed to take the edge off that margin, not remove it.

How acca insurance works

A typical acca insurance offer says: if exactly one leg of your accumulator loses, you get your stake back, usually up to a cap and usually as a free bet rather than cash. The details vary by bookmaker, so read them, but these are the terms to look for:

  • Minimum legs: often five or more.
  • Minimum odds per leg: for example 1.20 or 1.30, so very short favourites do not count.
  • The cap: the most you can get back.
  • Free bet or cash: a free bet is worth less than cash, because with a free bet you normally keep only the winnings, not the stake.
  • Eligible markets: often only pre-match bets on selected leagues or markets.

What acca insurance is worth: a worked example

Take a five-leg acca, each leg a fair 50:50 chance priced at 1.90, for a £10 stake. The acca pays about 24.8 times your stake.

  • Chance all five win: 0.5 to the power of five, about 3.1%.
  • Chance exactly one leg loses: five ways for that to happen, each 3.1%, so about 15.6%.

So about one acca in six and a half gives you a £10 free bet back. A free bet typically returns about 70% to 80% of its face value as cash if you use it on sensible prices, so call it £7.50. That is worth about 15.6% × £7.50 = £1.17 per £10 acca. Without the insurance, this acca returns on average about £7.75 for every £10 staked (3.1% × £248). With the insurance it is about £8.92. Better, but still below the £10 you put in.

That is the honest summary: acca insurance is worth something, often around a tenth of your stake on a five-fold, but on its own it rarely turns a bad acca into a good one.

How odds boosts work

An odds boost raises the price on a selection or an acca: a 'price boost' on one result, or a percentage boost on accumulator winnings (for example 10% extra on a five-fold). The question is always the same: is the boosted price above the fair price?

  • If a team's fair chance is 50% (fair price 2.00), a boost from 1.85 to 2.10 takes the price above fair. That is a genuinely good bet.
  • If the fair chance is 40% (fair price 2.50), the same boost from 1.85 to 2.10 is still below fair. It is a smaller loss, not a winner.

To find the fair chance, use a sharp bookmaker's or the exchange's price with the margin removed. Our guide to betting odds shows how.

Percentage acca boosts work the same way as insurance: they add something to every winning acca, but because accas win rarely, a 10% boost on winnings is worth much less than 10% of your stake.

A quick checklist

  1. Read the terms: minimum legs and odds, the cap, and whether you get cash or a free bet.
  2. Price the legs: are they fair on their own? Insurance does not fix legs priced well below fair.
  3. Do the sum: chance of one leg losing × the cash value of the refund, or the boosted price against the fair price.
  4. Keep accas small: fewer legs means less margin stacked up, and insurance or a boost then covers more of it.
