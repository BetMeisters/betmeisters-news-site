How to read a racecard: form figures, weights, ratings, headgear and the symbols that matter

A racecard packs a horse's whole story into one line. Here is what every part of it means, using a real handicap at Kempton tonight, and how to use it without getting lost in the detail.

Horse racingSports betting
By the BetMeisters.news desk18 November 20269 min read

A racecard looks like a code the first time you see one: strings of numbers and letters, weights in stones and pounds, odd symbols after a horse's name. But every part of it means something simple, and once you know the code you can read a horse's recent career in a few seconds. This guide goes through a card from top to bottom, using a real race: the 7.00pm at Kempton tonight, Wednesday 18 November (10.00pm Kampala, 2.00pm ET), a Class 2 handicap over a mile on the all-weather.

Racecards look slightly different in a newspaper, on a racecourse programme and on a betting site, but the information is the same. 18+.

The race heading

Before the runners, the card tells you what kind of race it is. For tonight's Kempton race it reads, in short: 7.00 · London Mile Series Qualifier Handicap · Class 2 · 0-105 · 3yo+ · 1m · Polytrack, standard to slow · 14 runners · £13,400.

  • Class runs from 1 (the best races, including Group and Listed races on the Flat and Graded races over jumps) down to 7. Class 2 is a good-quality race.
  • 0-105 is the rating band: only horses with an official rating up to 105 can run (a horse a pound or two above can still get in, carrying extra weight). You only see a band in handicaps.
  • 3yo+ means horses aged three or older.
  • Distance is in miles, furlongs and yards. A furlong is 220 yards, so 8 furlongs make a mile; a card might say 1m7f182y, which is just short of two miles.
  • Going is the official description of the ground: from heavy and soft through good to firm on turf, and standard, standard to slow or slow on all-weather surfaces. It changes during the day, so check it on the morning of the race. At Hexham today it was updated to good, good to soft in places.
  • Race type: Flat, hurdle, chase (steeplechase), or a National Hunt flat race, known as a bumper.

Number and draw. The first number is the saddlecloth. The number in brackets is the draw, the starting stall. Draws only exist on the Flat, because jumps races start from a tape. Palladas is drawn 1, the stall nearest the inside rail; Ebt's Guard is drawn 11, out wide. Whether a low or high draw helps depends on the course and distance, and on some tracks it matters a lot.

Name and symbols. Small letters after a name show past wins: C means a course winner, D a distance winner, and CD a winner over this course and distance. BF means the horse was a beaten favourite last time. So Palladas has already won over a mile at Kempton, and started favourite and lost on his latest run. Letters in brackets are headgear (see below): Oliver Show wears (tp).

Age. In Britain and Ireland every horse has an official birthday of 1 January, so a three-year-old tonight is in its third calendar year of age, whatever its actual foaling date.

Trainer. Many cards also show how the trainer is doing. In the 14 days to Wednesday, Charlie Appleby had two winners from four runners (50%) and William Haggas two from seven (29%). Trainer form over a short period is a hint, not proof: a small number of runners can make a strike rate look very good or very bad.

Form figures: reading the string

The form figures are a horse's recent finishing positions, read from left to right, with the most recent run on the right. Numbers 1 to 9 are finishing positions, and 0 means the horse finished tenth or worse. Two symbols mark breaks: a hyphen (-) separates seasons, and a slash (/) means a longer gap, two seasons or more.

Letters show runs where the horse did not finish, which mostly happen over jumps:

  • F fell · U unseated rider · P pulled up · B brought down · R refused · S slipped up · V the race was void.

So in tonight's race:

  • Palladas, 114: won his first two races, then finished fourth last time.
  • Princling, -14563: the hyphen shows a new season started before his first run this year. Since then he has finished 1st, 4th, 5th, 6th and, last time, 3rd.
  • Ebt's Guard, 944332: ninth, two fourths, two thirds and a second. Consistent, but without a win in those six runs.
  • Oliver Show, 442766: two fourths and a second, then 7th and two 6ths: his recent form has tailed off.

Form figures tell you where a horse finished, not how good the race was or how far it was beaten. A third in a Class 2 handicap can be better form than a win in Class 6. That is why form figures are a starting point, not the whole story.

Days since the last run

Most cards show a number of days since the horse last ran. Tonight Oliver Show ran 20 days ago and Palladas 26; Princling has had 47 days off. Some cards show a second figure in brackets for a run under a different code, for example a jumps horse that last ran on the Flat. A long break is not a bad sign in itself, but it is a question: some horses go well fresh, others need a run.

Weight, official ratings and how a handicap works

Weights are shown in stones and pounds. A stone is 14 pounds, so 10-0 is 140lb and 9-3 is 129lb. The weight includes the jockey and the saddle, with lead added to reach the right figure.

The OR column is the horse's official rating, set by the British Horseracing Authority's handicappers and updated every week. In a handicap, the weights are set from those ratings: the highest-rated horse carries top weight, and the others carry less in proportion, so that in theory every horse has the same chance. Each point of rating is worth one pound.

You can see it working in tonight's race. Ebt's Guard (rated 104) carries 10-0. Ebt's Guard and the four-year-old filly Chantilly Lace (103) are a pound apart in both rating and weight (139lb). The three-year-olds get a little more: Princling is rated 14 pounds below Ebt's Guard but carries 16 pounds less. The extra two pounds is the weight-for-age allowance, which younger horses receive against older ones.

In races that are not handicaps (maidens, novice races, Group races), weights are set by the race conditions instead: by age and sex, plus penalties for previous wins. There, a horse with a much higher rating can be meeting rivals on level weights, which is why the best horse often wins and the favourites are shorter.

The jockey's claim

A number in brackets after a jockey's name is a claim: a weight allowance for a less experienced rider. Tonight Rhys Elliott (5) and Toby Moore (7) are claiming. Ebt's Guard is allotted 10-0 but actually carries 5lb less, 9-9; Oliver Show carries 7lb less than his 8-13. The claim reduces as the rider wins more races. A good young rider with a claim can be an advantage, because the horse carries less weight than its rating says it should.

Headgear codes

Headgear is equipment worn to help a horse concentrate or settle. The common codes are:

  • b blinkers · v visor · h hood · t tongue-tie · p cheekpieces · e/s eye shield · e/c eye cover.

A 1 after the code means the horse is wearing it for the first time, for example b1 for first-time blinkers. That is worth noticing: a trainer only changes equipment when he or she thinks it might help. Oliver Show's (tp) means a tongue-tie and cheekpieces.

The market

The last column on a betting site is the price. On Tuesday evening Palladas was about 13/8 favourite for tonight's race; by 7.30am Kampala on Wednesday he was about 6/5, with Princling about 9/4. A price is just the market's view of the chance (6/5 is about 45% before the bookmaker's margin, 9/4 about 31%), and it changes through the day. Our guide to betting odds shows how to turn any price into a chance.

If a horse is withdrawn after you have taken a price, a Rule 4 deduction may be taken from your winnings, because the rest of the field has a better chance. Our guide to best odds guaranteed and extra places explains how deductions and the starting price work.

A simple way to use a racecard

  1. Start with the race. Class, distance, going and whether it is a handicap tell you what kind of contest it is.
  2. Look for the right symbols. C, D and CD show horses that have won in similar conditions; BF and first-time headgear are worth a second look.
  3. Read the form string from the right. The last two or three runs matter most, and a hyphen or slash tells you about a break.
  4. In a handicap, compare rating and weight. A horse well in at the weights, or ridden by a good claimer, can carry less than its ability suggests.
  5. Check the going and the draw on the day. Both change, and both can matter more than the form.
  6. Then look at the price. The question is never just 'can it win?' but 'is the price bigger than its chance?'

Common mistakes

  • Reading form left to right as if the first figure were the latest. It is the other way round.
  • Treating every win the same. A win in a weak race is not the same as a place in a strong one.
  • Ignoring the going. Some horses act on soft ground and others do not, and form on one may say little about the other.
  • Forgetting the claim. The weight shown is not always the weight carried.
  • Overrating short-term trainer form. Two winners from four runners is a good week, not a guarantee.

FAQs

What does 0 mean in form figures? The horse finished tenth or worse.

Why do some form strings have letters? They show runs where the horse did not finish: F fell, U unseated rider, P pulled up, B brought down, R refused.

What is the OR? The official rating, the BHA's measure of a horse's ability in pounds. In handicaps it sets the weight carried.

What does CD mean? The horse has won over this course and distance before.

Does the draw matter over jumps? No. Jumps races start from a tape, so there is no draw.
