Tennis betting explained: match odds, set betting, games handicaps and what happens if a player retires

Tennis has no draw, a clear scoring system and markets on every set and game. Here is how the main markets settle, how to read a games handicap, and why retirement rules differ from one bookmaker to the next.

TennisSports betting
By the BetMeisters.news desk22 November 20267 min read

Tennis is one of the easiest sports to bet on in one way, because there is no draw: somebody always wins. It is also one of the trickiest, because the scoring creates lots of different markets and the rules for an unfinished match are not the same everywhere.

The format decides a lot

Most ATP and WTA matches are best of three sets. Men's singles at the four Grand Slams are best of five. A set is won by the first player to six games with a two-game lead; at 6-6 a tie-break decides it, and in the deciding set at the Grand Slams the tie-break goes to 10 points. Best of five favours the stronger player, because an upset needs more to go right, so the same two players can be priced differently at a Slam and at a regular tour event.

Match odds (money line)

You pick the winner. With two outcomes, turning prices into chances is simple: one divided by the price for each player, and the two figures add up to a little over 100%; the extra is the bookmaker's margin. A player at 1.50 against an opponent at 2.62 works out at about 66.7% and 38.2%, a total of 104.9%, so the margin is about 5%. Remove it and the fair chances are about 64% and 36%.

Set betting

You pick the exact score in sets: 2-0, 2-1, 1-2 or 0-2 in a best-of-three match, and from 3-0 to 0-3 in best of five. Prices are bigger because you are calling the shape of the match, not just the winner.

Games handicap

A games handicap adds or takes away games from a player's total across the match. If you back a player at -3.5 games, they must win at least four more games than their opponent. A 6-4, 6-4 win is 12 games to 8, a margin of four, so -3.5 wins; 7-6, 6-4 is 13 to 10, a margin of three, so it loses. The underdog at +3.5 games is the mirror image. Whole-number handicaps can push, exactly as in football.

Total games

Over or under a number of games in the match, for example 21.5. Close matches with tie-breaks go over; one-sided matches go under. Serving strength matters: big servers hold more often and play more tie-breaks.

Other markets

  • First set winner: settles once the first set is complete, whatever happens after.
  • Set handicap: for example -1.5 sets, which needs a straight-sets win.
  • Tie-break in the match: yes or no.

When a player retires: check the rules

Retirements are common in tennis, and bookmakers settle them in different ways. A comparison by Flashscore (last updated October 2025) found two main groups among UK bookmakers:

  • Full match completion: match bets are void unless the match is played to the end. The list included bet365, William Hill, Betway and BetVictor.
  • First set completion: if at least one set has been completed, the player who goes through is paid as the winner. The list included Ladbrokes, Coral, Betfred, Unibet and BetMGM.

Pinnacle uses the one-set rule for match bets but voids games handicaps, total games and set betting when a player retires. US sportsbooks also differ: according to the Action Network, FanDuel and BetMGM need one completed set, while Caesars needs the full match. The same retirement can therefore be a winner, a void or a loser depending on where the bet was placed, so read the tennis rules before you bet, especially on players with an injury history.

How to judge a price

Compare the price with a sharp market with the margin removed. If the fair chance of a player winning is 60%, the fair price is 1 ÷ 0.60 = 1.67. A price of 1.75 is about 5% above fair (1.75 × 0.60 = 1.05); a price of 1.60 is about 4% below. Surface, recent workload and travel between tournaments move tennis prices more than headlines do.
