Betting odds explained: fractional, decimal and American odds, and what they mean as a chance

Every price is a chance in disguise. Here is how to read fractional, decimal and American odds, convert between them, read each-way terms and price moves, and spot how much margin a bookmaker is charging.

FootballHorse racingSports betting
By the BetMeisters.news desk16 November 20268 min read

Odds look like three different languages: 5/2 in a betting shop, 3.50 on an exchange, +250 on an American app. They all say the same two things: how much you win if your bet comes in, and what chance the bookmaker is putting on it happening. Once you can read odds as a chance, you can tell a fair price from a poor one. That is the single most useful skill in betting.

Fractional odds: the British way

Fractional odds show your profit relative to your stake, without the stake. At 5/2 you win £5 for every £2 staked, so a £10 bet wins £25 profit and returns £35 in total, exactly the same as decimal 3.50.

  • Profit = stake × (top number ÷ bottom number)
  • To convert to decimal: divide the top by the bottom and add 1. So 5/2 = 2.5 + 1 = 3.50.

Odds-on prices are written the other way round: 4/6 means you win £4 for every £6 staked, which is decimal 1.67. 'Evens' is 1/1.

American odds: plus and minus

American (moneyline) odds use £100 or $100 as the base.

  • Positive odds (+250) show the profit on a 100 stake. +250 means 100 wins 250, which is decimal 3.50.
  • Negative odds (−150) show how much you must stake to win 100. −150 means you stake 150 to win 100, which is decimal 1.67.

To convert: for positive odds, decimal = 1 + (odds ÷ 100). For negative odds, decimal = 1 + (100 ÷ the number without the minus sign).

Turning odds into a chance

This is the part most punters skip. Every price implies a probability:

Implied chance = 1 ÷ decimal odds

So 2.00 implies 50%, 4.00 implies 25% and 1.25 implies 80%. If you think something happens more often than the price implies, the price is good value for you. If it happens less often, the bookmaker has the better of it.

A worked example: a team is priced at 2.50, which implies 40%. If the honest chance of that team winning is 45%, then over many bets like this you would expect to win: 45% of the time you collect 2.50, which averages 1.125 back for every 1 staked, a 12.5% edge. If the true chance is only 35%, you average 0.875 back, a 12.5% loss.

The overround: how bookmakers make money

Add up the implied chances for every outcome in a market and you will get more than 100%. The extra is the bookmaker's margin, also called the overround or 'vig'.

Take a football match priced Home 2.10, Draw 3.40, Away 3.60. The implied chances are 47.6% + 29.4% + 27.8% = 104.8%. That 4.8% is the margin: the bookmaker's built-in edge over everyone betting on that market.

Margins vary a lot. On BetMeisters' own price data for 16 November 2026, the average margin on main markets ran from about 8.5% at bet365 to about 10.2% at Paddy Power across the four big UK firms we track. Sharp bookmakers and exchanges run much lower margins, which is why we use them to work out fair prices.

Odds-on, evens and odds-against

Three phrases you will hear all the time:

  • Evens (1/1, decimal 2.00, American +100) means you win the same as you stake. The implied chance is exactly 50%.
  • Odds-against means the price is bigger than evens: 3/1, 4.00, +300. You win more than you stake, and the implied chance is under 50%.
  • Odds-on means the price is shorter than evens: 1/2, 1.50, −200. You win less than you stake, and the implied chance is over 50%. Fractional odds-on prices are often said backwards in a betting shop: 1/2 is called "two to one on".

Odds-on does not mean safe. A 1/2 shot is about a 67% chance before the margin, so it loses about one time in three. String three of them together in a treble and the chance of all three winning is about 30%.

Each-way odds: the place fraction

In horse racing and golf you will often see a price like 10/1 each way, 1/5 three places. An each-way bet is two bets of the same size: one to win, and one to finish in the places. The place part pays at a fraction of the win odds.

Take a £5 each-way bet (£10 in total) at 10/1, with a 1/5 place fraction and three places paid:

  • If the horse wins, the win part returns £55 (£50 profit plus the £5 stake) and the place part returns £15 (10/1 × 1/5 = 2/1, so £10 profit plus £5). Total return £70.
  • If it finishes second or third, the win part loses and the place part returns £15. Total return £15, so £5 profit on £10 staked.
  • If it finishes fourth or worse, both parts lose.

The number of places and the fraction depend on the race type and the number of runners, and bookmakers sometimes offer extra places on big races. Those terms decide whether an each-way bet is good value, so always check them, not just the win price.

Early prices, starting price and why odds move

Odds are not fixed. They move as money comes in, as team news or non-runners are announced, and as bookmakers react to each other.

  • An early price is a price you take before the day or before the market settles. Thin early markets move the most.
  • In UK and Irish racing, the starting price (SP) is the official price at the off. If you bet "at SP" you get whatever that turns out to be.
  • Some bookmakers offer best odds guaranteed on racing: if you take a price and the SP is bigger, you are paid at the SP.
  • When a horse is withdrawn late, bookmakers can apply a Rule 4 deduction to winning bets on the rest of the field at earlier prices, because the race became easier to win.

A move in the price is a move in the market's chance. When a horse goes from 5/4 (44% implied) to 4/5 (56% implied), the market now rates it much more highly. Watching how far and how fast a price moves tells you what the rest of the market thinks.

Odds on handicaps and totals

The same rules apply to every market, not just who wins. On a football total of Over 2.5 goals at 1.80, the implied chance is 1 ÷ 1.80 = 55.6%. On a basketball spread of −4.5 at 1.91 (American −110), the implied chance is 52.4%. Two-way markets like these are usually priced near 1.91 on each side, which is a margin of about 4.8%. That is why American bettors talk about needing to win about 52.4% of bets at −110 just to break even.

Some lines can land exactly. On a whole-number line such as Over 3 goals or −2 points, a result of exactly three goals or a two-point win returns your stake. Quarter lines like Over 2.25 split your stake across two lines, so you can win or lose half. Our guide to Asian handicaps covers these in detail.

Common mistakes with odds

  • Confusing return and profit. Decimal odds include your stake; fractional odds do not.
  • Thinking a short price is a safe bet. A 1.25 shot still loses one time in five.
  • Ignoring the margin. Two bookmakers can both have a favourite at 'about 2/1', but 2.10 against 2.25 is a big difference over hundreds of bets.
  • Chasing long odds. Big prices are exciting, but they usually carry the biggest margins.
